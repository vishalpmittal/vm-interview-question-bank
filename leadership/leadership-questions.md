# Leadership Interview Questions

Consolidated question bank for engineering leadership and management interviews (tech leads, EMs, senior+ managers). Organized by competency area, with section-level guidance for evaluating responses.

## How to Use

Pick 2-3 competency areas relevant to the role and level. Select 1-2 questions per area. Use the section-level "What to Listen For," "Follow-ups," and "Red Flags" to guide evaluation across all questions in that section.

## STAR-Method Evaluation

When evaluating answers, assess each component:

| Component | Strong Signal | Weak Signal |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|
| **Situation** | Specific context with stakes and constraints | Vague or hypothetical scenario |
| **Task** | Clear ownership and scope of their responsibility | Unclear what was theirs vs. the team's |
| **Action** | Concrete steps they personally took | "We did X" without clarifying their role |
| **Result** | Measurable outcome or specific impact | No resolution or only positive spin |

A strong answer names specifics: team size, timeline, what broke, what they tried, what happened. A weak answer stays abstract and uses "we" throughout without distinguishing personal contribution.

## People Management & Performance

Managing performance across the spectrum — underperformers, high performers, struggling individuals, and the messy people problems that consume real time.

### Questions

1. Tell me about a time you had to manage a poor performer on your team. What did you do?
2. Describe a situation where a team member was having difficulty performing a specific task. How did you help?
3. Tell me about a time you had to manage a difficult high performer.
4. Describe a people-related problem you had to spend a lot of time on. What made it hard?

**What to listen for:**
- Early identification of performance issues, not waiting until they become crises
- Direct, private, specific feedback grounded in observed behavior and impact
- A clear plan with milestones, expectations, and timelines — not just "more coaching"
- Distinguishing skill gaps from will gaps, and tailoring response accordingly
- Holding high performers to behavioral standards, not just output standards
- Willingness to make hard decisions (PIPs, role changes, exits) when warranted
- Investment in growth before escalation; partnership with HR/skip-level when appropriate
- Reflection on what they could have spotted or done earlier

**Follow-ups:**
- When did you first notice the issue, and what did you do about it?
- What was the person's perspective, and how did you account for it?
- What specific expectations did you set, and how did you measure progress?
- How did you handle it if it didn't improve?
- What did the rest of the team see, and how did you protect team morale?
- What would you do differently?

**Red flags:**
- Avoided the conversation until it became HR's problem
- Tolerates bad behavior from high performers because of their output
- "Coached" via vague hints rather than direct feedback
- No plan, no measurable expectations, no follow-through
- Frames the person as the entire problem with no self-reflection on management
- Cannot describe a hard people decision they actually made
- Treats people problems as distractions from "real work" rather than core management

## Coaching, Development & 1:1s

Growing individuals, structuring feedback loops, and using 1:1s as the primary management tool.

### Questions

1. Tell me about a time your structured 1:1 meetings weren't working. How did you change them moving forward?
2. Describe a time you developed and retained a team member who was at risk of leaving or stagnating.

**What to listen for:**
- Treating 1:1s as the report's time, not a status update for the manager
- Awareness of when a format is producing rapport but no growth (or vice versa)
- Tailoring coaching to the individual — career stage, learning style, motivation
- Concrete development plans tied to the person's goals, not just team needs
- Creating stretch opportunities and visibility, not just protecting people from hard work
- Retention through real career investment, not just compensation or perks
- Willingness to lose someone to a better opportunity if they've outgrown the role
- Specific outcomes — promotions, scope expansions, skill milestones, retention

**Follow-ups:**
- What signal told you the 1:1s weren't working?
- What format did you try, and how did you measure whether the new one worked?
- How did you understand what the person actually wanted from their career?
- What did you invest in (time, opportunities, sponsorship) and why?
- Did they stay, and was that the right outcome for them?

**Red flags:**
- 1:1s are status meetings dominated by the manager's agenda
- Cancels or reschedules 1:1s frequently — signals deprioritization
- Generic development advice not tied to the individual
- "Retention" framed only as counter-offers or perks
- Holds people back from internal moves to protect their own team
- Cannot name a specific person they grew, or only takes credit for others' work
- No reflection on what the report actually wanted vs. what the manager assumed

## Team Leadership & Motivation

Leading teams through hard moments — demoralizing events, performance dips, reorgs, and process change.

### Questions

1. Tell me about a time you had to motivate a team after a demoralizing event (e.g., a failed launch, layoff, attrition, scope cut).
2. Describe a time you had to motivate your team to perform at a higher level.
3. Tell me about a time you led your team through significant change — reorg, process change, leadership transition. How did you handle it?

**What to listen for:**
- Acknowledging the reality of the moment rather than papering over it
- Listening before acting — understanding what the team is actually feeling
- Distinguishing motivation problems (mission/clarity/trust) from performance problems (skill/structure/load)
- Concrete actions, not just speeches — adjusting scope, removing blockers, changing structure
- Transparency about what's known, unknown, and out of their control
- Protecting the team from upstream noise while keeping them informed
- Sustained follow-through after the initial moment passes
- Honest reflection on what worked, what didn't, and team-trust impact

**Follow-ups:**
- How did you find out the team was struggling / disengaged?
- What did the team tell you they needed vs. what you thought they needed?
- What specifically did you change about the work, structure, or environment?
- How did you measure whether things actually improved?
- What did you do for individuals vs. the team as a whole?

**Red flags:**
- "Motivated" the team with a pep talk, free lunch, or off-site and called it done
- Pushed harder without diagnosing why performance dropped
- Hid bad news from the team or sugarcoated change
- Took on all the emotional labor themselves and burned out
- Cannot describe a specific change they made to structure, process, or scope
- Treats motivation as the team's problem to solve, not the manager's
- Came across as performative rather than genuine

## Org Strategy & Alignment

Translating strategy down, surfacing misalignment up, keeping the team pointed at what actually matters.

### Questions

1. Tell me about a time you had to communicate a significant strategy change from leadership to your team.
2. Describe a situation where there was misalignment between your team's goals and the company's goals. What did you do?

**What to listen for:**
- Genuine engagement with the strategy themselves before relaying it — not parroting
- Translating company-level rationale into what it means for this specific team
- Honesty about what they agreed with, disagreed with, and committed to anyway
- Surfacing misalignment early and upward with data, not just complaining sideways
- Willingness to push back on leadership when the team's work is being misdirected
- Renegotiating scope, goals, or headcount rather than silently absorbing mismatch
- Protecting the team from constant pivots while still adapting when needed
- Outcome: realignment, scope change, or a clear-eyed disagree-and-commit

**Follow-ups:**
- How did you make sense of the strategy yourself before bringing it to the team?
- What questions did the team have that you couldn't immediately answer?
- How did you handle parts of the strategy you disagreed with?
- What did you escalate, to whom, and what evidence did you bring?
- How did the team's work actually change as a result?

**Red flags:**
- Relayed the strategy as a corporate message without translation or buy-in
- Hid their own disagreement and then undermined the strategy privately
- Vented sideways to peers instead of escalating to decision-makers
- Kept executing on misaligned goals because "leadership decided"
- No evidence brought to the escalation — just opinion
- Cannot describe a time they got leadership to actually change course
- Team learned about strategy changes from other sources first

## Managing Up & Cross-Level Dynamics

Being managed, evaluating managers, and handling moments when reports go over your head.

### Questions

1. Tell me about a time you had to evaluate the performance of an engineering manager who reported to you.
2. Describe a time when one of your reports reported something about you to upper management. How did you find out, and how did you handle it?

**What to listen for:**
- Evaluating EMs on team outcomes, people development, and judgment — not activity
- Looking at leading indicators (attrition, engagement, hiring quality, growth) not just delivery
- Skip-level signal-gathering done consistently, not just when there's a problem
- Calm, curious response to skip-level escalations — not defensiveness or retaliation
- Genuine reflection on what the report's concern revealed about their own management
- Repairing trust with the report directly, not just managing upward
- Distinguishing legitimate escalations from triangulation, and addressing each appropriately
- Outcome: changed behavior, rebuilt trust, and clear feedback loop going forward

**Follow-ups:**
- What were the criteria you used to evaluate the EM, and how did you weight them?
- How did you separate the EM's contribution from the team's underlying conditions?
- When you heard about the escalation, what was your first reaction vs. what you actually did?
- Did you talk to the report directly? How did that conversation go?
- What did you change about how you managed afterward?
- How did the relationship recover (or not)?

**Red flags:**
- Evaluates EMs primarily on their team's delivery, ignoring people and judgment
- Skip-levels only happen reactively when something is already wrong
- Reacted to a skip-level escalation with defensiveness, suspicion, or retaliation
- Tried to figure out "who told" instead of engaging with the substance
- Cannot describe what they learned about themselves from the escalation
- Avoided the direct conversation with the report afterward
- No change in behavior; treats the incident as a one-off rather than signal

## Leadership Philosophy & Approach

How the candidate actually operates day-to-day as a leader — coaching, 1:1s, goals, performance, prioritization, culture. These are "how do you" questions rather than STAR; evaluation focuses on operational specifics and adaptation, not narrative.

### Questions

1. How do you coach and develop your engineering team?
2. How do you structure your one-on-one meetings?
3. How do you set goals for the engineering team?
4. How do you manage your team's performance?
5. How do you prioritize features for your team?
6. How do you mentor top talent?
7. What is your leadership style? Give specific examples.
8. What do you think are the most important engineering manager skills?
9. How do you measure your team's success, and how do you report it?
10. Describe your team's culture.

**What to listen for:**
- Concrete operating practices (cadences, agendas, rubrics, signals tracked) — not platitudes
- Adaptation to context: senior IC vs. new grad, mature team vs. forming team
- Awareness of tradeoffs in their approach, not just advocacy for it
- Distinct philosophy that's their own, with mentors/experiences that shaped it
- Balance across people, process, and outcomes — not over-indexed on one
- Measurement: how they know their approach is working
- Examples of changing their approach based on evidence
- Culture description grounded in observable behaviors, not aspirational labels

**Follow-ups:**
- Can you give a recent example where this worked, and one where it didn't?
- How does your approach change for a senior engineer vs. a new grad?
- What signals tell you it's not working, and what do you do?
- What would you change about your approach today vs. five years ago?
- What books, mentors, or experiences shaped this?

**Red flags:**
- Recites a textbook framework with no personal adaptation or examples
- Cannot describe a time their approach didn't work
- One-size-fits-all answers ignoring team and individual context
- Vague principles ("be supportive", "communicate well") with no operational specifics
- Cannot articulate how they'd measure whether their approach is working
- Team culture described in aspirational labels rather than observed behaviors
- Treats coaching, hiring, or feedback as things to delegate or automate

## Hiring, Team Building & Credibility

How the candidate builds and inherits teams — hiring process, building high performance, establishing credibility when they didn't pick the team.

### Questions

1. How do you hire?
2. How do you build a high-performing engineering team?
3. How do you build credibility with new reports on a team you haven't built yourself?

**What to listen for:**
- A repeatable, evaluated hiring process (signals, rubrics, calibration) — not vibes
- A working definition of "high-performing" before claiming to build one
- Listening before changing, when stepping into an existing team
- Earning credibility through behavior and small wins, not credentials or proclamations
- Investment in psychological safety, clear roles, and feedback loops as foundations
- Awareness of team composition and complementary skills, not just individual hires
- Recognizing when to remove people, not just add them
- Patience with the first 30/60/90 days when inheriting a team

**Follow-ups:**
- What's the most common reason your hires don't work out, and how do you guard against it?
- What did you change about your process after a bad hire?
- When you inherit a team, what do you do in your first 30 days vs. 90 days?
- What's a credibility mistake you've made stepping into a new team?
- How do you tell whether a team is high-performing vs. just busy?

**Red flags:**
- "I hire smart people" with no actual process, signals, or rubric
- Tries to prove themselves by making big changes in the first 30 days
- Conflates "high-performing" with "agreeable" or "hardworking"
- No mention of psychological safety, team dynamics, or culture
- Cannot describe a hire they got wrong and what they learned
- Treats an inherited team as a problem to fix rather than people to understand
- Earns credibility through demos of expertise rather than enabling the team

## Self-Awareness & Reflection

Questions that probe the candidate's view of themselves, their feedback history, and their pattern recognition across teams and failures.

### Questions

1. What are your strengths and weaknesses?
2. What would a co-worker say about you? Share positive and negative feedback you've received.
3. What are the best and worst performing teams you've been on? What made them so?
4. Tell me about a project failure.
5. Which was your most effective team so far? What made them effective, and what methods did you use?

**What to listen for:**
- Specific, non-generic strengths and weaknesses with recent examples
- Weaknesses that are real (not humblebrags) and being actively worked on
- Plausible, pointed paraphrases of feedback they've received — both kinds
- Ability to articulate what specifically made a team effective or dysfunctional
- Ownership of their own role in failures, not just describing what others did
- Pattern recognition across teams — what they look for now vs. earlier in career
- Comfort discussing failure without bravado or excessive self-flagellation
- Concrete behavioral changes that stuck after each lesson

**Follow-ups:**
- What's a specific recent example where the weakness showed up?
- What are you doing about it, and how do you know it's working?
- What feedback have you received that genuinely surprised you?
- What did the dysfunctional team teach you about your own contribution to it?
- What patterns do you now look for that you missed earlier in your career?

**Red flags:**
- "Weakness" is a humblebrag (perfectionism, caring too much, working too hard)
- Cannot recall specific feedback, or only positive feedback
- Best team description is generic ("great culture, talented people") with no specifics
- Worst team is entirely other people's fault
- Project failure is framed as bad luck, bad market, or wrong stakeholders
- No evidence of behavioral change after the lessons
- Reflection sounds rehearsed rather than genuine

## Motivation & Role Fit

Why this company, this role, this moment. These are candidate-side questions — evaluation focuses on specificity, research, and authenticity of fit.

### Questions

1. Why do you want to work here?
2. What do you want to work on at this team?
3. How does the mission statement of our company resonate with you?
4. Write the job description for the position you are interviewing for.

**What to listen for:**
- Specific reasons grounded in research about the company, team, or role
- Authentic motivation tied to their career arc, not generic ambition
- Genuine engagement with the mission, including parts they'd push back on
- Clear-eyed view of the role's actual responsibilities and tradeoffs, not an idealized version
- A JD that reflects accurate understanding of scope, audience, and team context
- Willingness to ask clarifying questions when context is missing
- Match between what they want and what the role actually offers
- Curiosity about constraints and culture, not just perks and growth

**Follow-ups:**
- What about this role excites you most, and what concerns you?
- What part of our mission would you most want to challenge?
- Where did your JD diverge from how we'd describe it, and why?
- What would have to be true in year one for this to feel like a great decision?
- What's a company or role you turned down, and why?

**Red flags:**
- Generic answers that could apply to any company ("great culture, smart people")
- No research evidence — hasn't read recent product, blog, or announcements
- Mission resonance sounds performative, with no specific connection
- Cannot articulate the role's real responsibilities or tradeoffs
- JD reads like a template with no thought to actual team scope
- Wants the role for title / comp / optionality rather than the work
- No mention of what they want to learn, build, or change
